NANA WALLET — MASTER PRODUCT & PITCH CONTEXT

1. What Nana is

Nana Wallet is a voice-first agentic financial account, initially designed for seniors and people with limited mobility.

The core idea is simple:

People shouldn’t have to adapt to financial technology. Financial technology should adapt to them.

Instead of navigating menus, typing addresses, understanding wallets, selecting networks or learning financial interfaces, a user should be able to express intent naturally:

“Hey Nana, send $20 to my grandson.”

Nana understands the intent, identifies the recipient, prepares the financial action, checks the relevant policies, presents what actually matters to the user and executes after the appropriate authorization.

The long-term product is not intended to feel like a crypto wallet.

Stablecoins, blockchain networks, gas, addresses, on/off-ramps and settlement should become infrastructure underneath the experience, not concepts the user needs to understand.

⸻

2. Where the idea came from

Nana did not begin as an abstract thesis about AI agents.

It emerged from several personal experiences that converged during the Aleph Hackathon.

Marcos’ grandmothers

As Marcos’ grandmothers got older, something familiar happened.

The family’s way of protecting them was gradually taking responsibilities away.

Someone else paid bills.

Someone else managed money.

Someone else handled financial decisions.

This reduced some risks, but also gradually reduced their independence.

One of his grandmothers was also the victim of a financial scam.

That made the security problem concrete rather than theoretical.

The insight was:

Protection often becomes control.

Families have legitimate reasons to intervene, particularly because older adults can be vulnerable to scams, but the existing solution is often to remove financial autonomy entirely.

Nana asks whether technology can provide protection without requiring that loss of independence.

⸻

3. Susi

Another experience came from a member of the team who had taught technology to Susi, an older woman with Parkinson’s.

Susi understood what she wanted to do.

The problem wasn’t understanding money.

The problem was interacting with technology.

Traditional payment applications require:

* navigating screens;
* tapping relatively small controls;
* typing;
* selecting recipients;
* reviewing complex information;
* moving through multiple interface states.

For someone with limited mobility, reducing five clicks to three does not necessarily solve the underlying problem.

That led to another question:

What if instead of reducing interactions, we rethink which interactions deserve to exist?

6. What the first prototype proved

The first Nana prototype was built during the Aleph Hackathon.

It demonstrated the core interaction:

user intent
    ↓
agent
    ↓
prepare transaction
    ↓
user confirmation
    ↓
wallet infrastructure
    ↓
onchain execution

The prototype used Tether WDK.

Nana executed 7 confirmed transactions on Ethereum Sepolia, ranging from 1 to 20 test USD₮.

The transactions were prepared through the agentic flow and confirmed by the user.

This matters because Nana was not only a Figma prototype or conversational demo.

The basic agent → wallet → transaction loop actually worked.

9. The second user

One of the most important discoveries after building Nana was that the product does not have only one user.

It has at least two roles.

End user

Initially:

* senior;
* person with limited mobility;
* person who benefits from assisted financial interaction.

They want:

Independence.

They primarily:

* speak;
* express intent;
* use money;
* confirm meaningful actions.

Responsible user

Potentially:

* son;
* daughter;
* family member;
* designated responsible person.

They want:

Peace of mind without having to become somebody else’s financial interface.

They may:

* assist with onboarding;
* configure relationships;
* configure policies;
* receive alerts;
* supervise exceptional activity;
* intervene when genuinely necessary.

The model therefore becomes:

A dual-user consumer financial product.

The current GTM hypothesis is responsible-led acquisition: the son, daughter or responsible person discovers Nana and helps bring the end user into the system. nana-modelo(1).pdf

Nana sits between two undesirable extremes.

The core principle is:

13. The difficult security problem

The obvious suspicious transaction is not actually the hardest case.

A huge transaction to an unknown recipient is easy to flag.

A sophisticated scam may look like:

$40 every week for two months to a “new account belonging to the grandson.”

Each transaction is individually plausible.

The pattern is what matters.

Therefore Nana’s long-term protection architecture potentially combines:

* deterministic policies;
* spending limits;
* trusted recipients;
* behavioral analysis;
* anomaly detection;
* contextual reasoning;
* escalation;
* human oversight.

This is still an area of product and technical research, not a solved component.

⸻

14. Agent autonomy

The first prototype required explicit confirmation before execution.

The next technical question is harder:

Can Nana safely perform routine financial actions within authority delegated by the user without requiring manual cryptographic signing for every transaction and without giving the agent unrestricted control of the wallet?

The direction currently being explored is:

Smart Account + programmable permissions + delegated execution/session keys + multisig escalation.

Conceptually:

USER
 ↓
delegates bounded authority
 ↓
NANA AGENT
 ↓
allowed recipient?
within spending limit?
within policy?
 ↓
YES ───────────────→ EXECUTE
NO / EXCEPTION
 ↓
HOLD
 ↓
additional authorization

The agent should never simply possess unrestricted authority over the user’s funds.

Authority should be:

* bounded;
* explicit;
* revocable;
* policy-constrained.

19. Onboarding tension

There is an unresolved product issue.

If the responsible person performs KYC and owns/funds the wallet, then the money may technically belong to the responsible person.

That undermines the independence thesis.

If the wallet belongs to the end user, the end user must participate in onboarding/KYC.

The likely direction is therefore not:

The responsible person does everything instead of them.

But:

The responsible person accompanies the difficult setup once so that the end user gets simplicity every day.

This remains an open product/legal question. nana-modelo(1).pdf

20. Nana Free

The core security proposition cannot be premium.

A free Nana account should demonstrate the complete core loop:

independence + protection.

Core protection may include:

* meaningful confirmation;
* basic policies;
* trusted recipients;
* anomaly detection;
* responsible relationship;
* alerts.

If protection only exists in premium, free Nana becomes merely a voice wallet.

That would hide the actual differentiation behind a paywall.

B. Nana Family

Banks, fintechs, wallets or insurers could eventually license Nana’s agentic accessibility / protection / family layer.
